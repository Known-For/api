Cybersecurity underinvestment has been a governance problem for a decade. Boards deferred investment on math that had made sense. If the effort to exploit a vulnerability was high, the probability of attack was low enough that acceptance was reasonable. Expensive fixes got sequenced and legacy systems got left alone — and for a decade, boards treated that as responsible stewardship. AI collapsed the effort side of that equation. Work that took specialist teams weeks now takes hours. The complexity of legacy systems — once protection, because it made them hard to attack — has become the attack surface itself. The calculus that justified a decade of deferral is dead. Every unpatched system is a realistic target. Every architecture decision you deferred is a live exposure. I've watched shifts like this before. When the assumption underneath a risk model breaks, the investment posture built on top of it breaks too. The organizations that move early look prescient in hindsight — but the move itself feels premature, because the old math hasn't fully gone cold. My colleagues Frank Ford, Andrew Cousins, Syed Ali, and Alexandra Juegelt lay out the full picture — the Mythos moment, the OT exposure, the investment gap — in Bain's new Brief on AI cybersecurity. The argument about why boards deferred in the first place is the part worth reading carefully. The deferral era is over. What the Brief actually names is what boards will be measured on now. Today, AI agents are genuinely capable — and still behaving like temporary workers. The work ends when the session does. Context doesn't carry and value doesn't compound. Most agents arrive when prompted, execute, and reset. They don't improve because they're deployed more. In organizational terms, the best description is a capable temp. That's a useful maturity signal. But a new class of long-running agents is beginning to emerge: systems designed not just to complete tasks but to maintain goals, preserve decisions, and accumulate domain-specific knowledge across time. They can resume work rather than restart it, track unresolved issues, and coordinate across people, tools, and systems over extended workflows. The distinction matters because as context builds, what the agent creates is no longer just operational throughput. It's institutional knowledge: how a specific supplier actually negotiates, which process exceptions succeed, why certain escalation patterns signal churn. That knowledge has always lived in people's heads and walked out the door with them. Long-running agents may be the first systems capable of capturing it systematically. My Bain colleagues and lay out the operating model implications in a new piece worth reading as you think through your agentic investments. The accumulation question is the one worth asking. The debate about AI and jobs keeps getting the question wrong. What most organizations are asking is whether AI will displace workers. The organizations that have actually deployed AI at scale have moved past that question — because the answer, in practice, looks more nuanced than either side of the debate. At , voice AI now handles orders in more than 700 drive-throughs. Employee satisfaction went up after deployment. Because instead of jobs disappearing, one of the worst parts of them did. AI takes the order. The employee handles everything else — and steps in as the exception handler when the AI gets stuck. The system performs better than either alone. This is the pattern we see across mature deployments: AI absorbs the rote, humans absorb the judgment, and the combination produces something neither could do independently. This is workforce redesign rather than displacement. Which tasks belong to the machine, which belong to the person, and how do you build the handoff between them. Most enterprises haven't gotten there yet. They're still in the pilot phase, still measuring AI against a standard of perfection it can't meet, still waiting to deploy until the technology is ready to work alone. The organizations pulling ahead have accepted that it won't work alone — and built accordingly. That little AI disclosure indicator appearing in your enterprise tools — the sparkle button, the glowing badge, whatever form it takes — is a trust marker. Understanding what it's measuring tells you more about where AI adoption really is than most maturity frameworks. We've spent three years living with hallucinations, double-checking outputs, and calibrating which AI claims to trust. That calibration process left an artifact in the UX. The disclosure badge is that residue — a signal that says "AI made this, and you should know before you rely on it." Consider what it takes for that badge to disappear. My thermostat has had AI in it for years. It adjusts temperature based on learned patterns, and I've never once asked for disclosure before accepting its recommendation. The trust is established. The AI is invisible. The thermostat and the enterprise tool have comparable AI capabilities. What separates them is three years of hallucination anxiety — built into one category of software, absent in the other. AI becomes infrastructure when that trust gap closes. The models will keep getting better on their own schedule. The badge disappears on a different one — slower, messier, and entirely human. The best measure of AI maturity isn't what the models can do. It's whether anyone still feels the need to tell you they're running.

Most quantum boardroom conversations end without an agenda. They end with a posture — "we're monitoring quantum developments," "we're taking it seriously". Neither statement produces a plan. The distinction matters because quantum creates three problem classes, each with a different urgency and a different cost of inaction. A generic posture misaddresses all three at once. The right response, for most leadership teams, has three parts. The first is to defend now. Post-quantum cryptography belongs on the enterprise risk agenda as a current priority. That means building visibility into cryptographic dependencies across the enterprise, identifying migration priorities, and mapping third-party exposure. This is the part of the quantum agenda that cannot wait. The second is to explore selectively. Most leadership teams do not need a wide portfolio of quantum pilots. They need a small number of focused efforts on high-value problems where the workload aligns with quantum's actual strengths — evaluated against the strongest available classical alternative. Each effort should be a targeted test: one specific problem, one clear classical benchmark, one honest evaluation. The third is to build options. For companies in simulation-relevant sectors — pharmaceuticals, advanced materials, energy — the right posture is modest investment in partnerships and early hardware collaborations. The goal is R&D workflows that are ready to integrate quantum subroutines when the technology matures. The companies that benefit most will not necessarily be those spending the most today. They will be the ones best positioned to move when the moment arrives. The most common failure on quantum is conflating the urgency of the three classes — treating all three as equally distant or equally immediate, when each has a different clock running. The organizations that get this right understand early which problem classes matter to their business, which ones to set aside, and what the distinction demands of them starting Monday morning. https://lnkd.in/gkymW7Xm

And in my experience, the organizations making progress are the ones where the CEO and board can answer each of them with specificity.

AI was supposed to remove people from the work. What's actually happening is the opposite — it's pulling engineers closer to it. One of the more telling signals of where enterprise AI really is right now is the rise of forward-deployed engineers. It's honestly a term of art — and different companies mean slightly different things by it — but the pattern is consistent. These aren't traditional pre-sales roles, and they're not classic post-implementation support either. They exist because modern AI systems, especially agentic ones, require much tighter coupling with real workflows than earlier technology ever did. As AI moves from generative to agentic — from single-shot answers to multi-step reasoning, planning, and action — the distance between the model and the work starts to matter enormously. Agents have to navigate ambiguous data, incomplete processes, and contextual decision-making that enterprises usually leave to humans. That gap between the research lab and the operating reality of a business is still large. And today, it's being bridged by skilled engineers embedded in the work itself — engineers who can translate intent into production outcomes and feed what they learn back into the system. What's interesting is what this tells us about the maturity curve. Forward-deployed engineers are a signal that AI has moved past demos and proofs of concept, but hasn't yet reached the point where these systems can operate independently at enterprise scale. We're in a phase where the technology and the people who understand the work have to be symbiotic. For leaders thinking about AI deployment, the implication is subtle but important. The talent question isn't just about "AI skills." It's about recognizing that some of the most valuable roles in this next phase sit right at the intersection of the models and the messy reality of how your business actually runs.

The question at Davos changed this year. It's no longer "what is AI" or even "are we getting ROI." The question I heard most from CEOs: "How do I bring my people along?" In a fireside chat with Andrew Ng and a dinner we hosted with CEOs navigating AI transformation, the conversations clustered around two themes: the organizational choices required to scale AI, and the personal investments leaders must make to guide their teams through this moment. On the organizational side, the "let a thousand flowers bloom" era is reaching its natural limits. Bottom-up experimentation built early momentum, but it rarely self-organizes into enterprise value. The focus is shifting to top-down prioritization of AI bets—identifying the workflows where transformation creates real competitive advantage, then allocating serious resources to make it happen. And a harder challenge is emerging: developing leaders who can manage two workforces. People and agents, working side by side. That means preserving trust, inspiration, and purpose for human teams while integrating AI systems into how work actually gets done. Most enterprises don't have a playbook for this yet. On the personal side, leading an AI-powered transformation demands real investment. Carving out dedicated learning time. Reverse-mentoring from tech-native colleagues. Role modeling curiosity rather than judgment. Andrew made a point that stuck with me: every executive should learn to code—not to become an engineer, but to rewire how we think about problems and systems. To understand the potential and limitations of the technology. Several CEOs talked about publicly celebrating their own failures as a way to normalize experimentation. Others emphasized the need to proactively shape AI conversations with their Boards rather than reacting to headlines. I left Davos energized and optimistic about where we're headed, with fresh perspective on how I lead Bain's own AI transformation and new ideas for how we support our clients in theirs. The theme of bringing people along through change is emerging for me as one of the defining ideas of the week and, I suspect, 2026. The technology is moving fast. The real work now is making sure our organizations and our people can move with it.

[3mo]

It's been fascinating to watch the world move past the "parlor trick" phase of artificial intelligence. Over the last three years, the conversation was often dominated by the wow factor—models winning math competitions or writing poetry. Leaders debated whether the technology really mattered and whether it was creating real value. That debate is largely over. Today, the conversation has shifted from novelty to something far more boring and far more consequential: how work gets done and who does it. AI is no longer just a "copilot" you bolt onto an application. It's becoming part of the fabric of the enterprise—embedded in workflows, shaping decisions, and increasingly acting as a new kind of digital labor. AI has graduated from experiment to a standard unit of work. Through agents, it now executes tasks, coordinates processes, and operates alongside humans across software development, customer support, and the sales back office. Not as a feature, but as part of how the enterprise runs. That shift changes how we should think about investment. When AI was an "app," you bought it like software: a license, a user, a login.
